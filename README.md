## What Is an Operating Agreement?

An operating agreement is the internal governing document of an LLC. It records who owns the company, how it is managed, how money moves in and out, and what happens if an owner leaves, becomes incapacitated, or the company is dissolved.

Think of it as the rulebook the owners agreed to *before* money, customers, or disagreements showed up. Most states do not require you to file it anywhere — it lives with your company records — but "not filed" does not mean "optional."

If you have no operating agreement, your LLC is governed by your state's default LLC statute. Those default rules were not written for your business and can produce results no owner actually wanted — especially on ownership, voting, and what happens on exit.

## Why Banks and the IRS Care About Having One

**Banks and fintechs.** Most US business banks, payment processors, and fintech platforms ask for your operating agreement when you open an account — for a multi-member LLC, almost always. It is how they verify:

- Who the owners are and what percentage each owns
- Who is authorized to open accounts, sign, and move money
- Whether the person applying actually has authority to act for the company

No agreement (or a sloppy one that doesn't match your Articles of Organization) is one of the most common reasons a non-resident founder's bank application stalls.

**The IRS and tax classification.** Your operating agreement helps establish:

- How the LLC is structured for tax purposes (a single-member LLC is a disregarded entity by default; a multi-member LLC is a partnership by default, unless you elect otherwise)
- Ownership percentages, which drive how income, losses, and distributions are allocated — including on Form 1065 and Schedules K-1 for multi-member LLCs
- That the LLC is a real, separately-run business — supporting the separation between you and the company that gives the LLC its liability protection

**Liability protection.** Courts look at whether an LLC was actually run as a separate entity. A signed operating agreement, kept with your records and followed in practice, is core evidence of that — alongside separate bank accounts, proper bookkeeping, and not mixing personal and company funds.

## Single-Member vs. Multi-Member: Key Differences

| Topic | Single-Member | Multi-Member |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | One member | Two or more members |
| Default US tax treatment | Disregarded entity | Partnership |
| Management | Member-managed (usually) | Member-managed or manager-managed |
| Voting | Sole member decides | By ownership % or per capita — must be spelled out |
| Distributions | To the sole member | Pro-rata by ownership % unless otherwise agreed |
| Transfer of ownership | Member sells/transfers interest | Restrictions, rights of first refusal, and buyout terms matter a lot |
| Exit / dispute provisions | Simple — succession and dissolution | Critical — deadlock, buy-sell, expulsion, valuation |
| Why the document matters most | Bank accounts, liability separation, succession | All of that, **plus** preventing partner disputes |

A single-member agreement is short because there is nobody to disagree with — its job is formality, authority, and continuity. A multi-member agreement is longer because its real job is deciding hard questions (money, control, exit) while everyone still gets along.

## How to Use These Templates

1. **Pick the right template** — single-member if you are the only owner; multi-member if there are two or more owners.
2. **Fill in every `[BRACKETED]` field.** Search the document for `[` to make sure nothing is left blank. Blanks in a signed agreement are worse than no agreement.
3. **Align it with your other records.** Company name, formation state, formation date, addresses, and ownership percentages must match your Articles of Organization / Certificate of Formation exactly.
4. **Choose your management structure deliberately:**
   - *Member-managed* — all members run day-to-day operations.
   - *Manager-managed* — members appoint one or more managers to run the company; members are more passive. (Offered as an option in the multi-member template.)
5. **Have it reviewed.** Especially for multi-member LLCs, foreign-owned LLCs, or any company taking outside investment, have a US business attorney review the completed draft (see the disclaimer above).
6. **Sign it.** Every member signs. Electronic signatures are generally accepted for internal documents, but wet-ink or a reputable e-sign tool with an audit trail is best practice.
7. **Store it with your company records** — with your Articles of Organization, EIN letter (CP 575), registered agent details, and meeting minutes. Give every member a copy.
8. **Do NOT file it with the state.** Operating agreements are internal documents. You do not submit them to the Secretary of State. You produce them when a bank, the IRS, a counterparty, or a court asks.
9. **Amend it when things change.** New member, changed ownership %, new manager, new address — record the change with a written amendment signed by the members, and keep the old version.

## What's Inside Each Template

Both templates cover, in standard legal-document format:

- Company formation details (name, state, effective date, purpose, duration)
- Principal place of business and registered agent
- Members and ownership percentages
- Management structure and authority
- Capital contributions (initial and additional)
- Allocations and distributions
- Voting and decision-making
- Transfer restrictions and buyout mechanics
- Dissolution and winding up
- Indemnification and limitation of liability
- Governing law, amendments, and miscellaneous provisions
- Signature blocks for all members (and managers, where applicable)

## For Non-Resident Founders

If you formed a US LLC from outside the United States, an operating agreement is not a nice-to-have — it is routinely the document that unblocks your US bank account, Stripe/PayPal verification, and marketplace onboarding. Keep a signed copy (PDF) ready to upload; platforms ask for it again and again.
